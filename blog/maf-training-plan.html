MAF Training Plan: An 8-Week Aerobic Base-Building Schedule

📅 July 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read 🏃 Fitness

Knowing your MAF heart rate is the easy part. The harder part is sticking with the slow pace for long enough to see it pay off — and knowing whether it's actually working. This is a concrete 8-week schedule built around the tool MAF training actually uses to measure progress: the MAF test, done on a set schedule so your numbers mean something.

Before you start

You'll need your MAF heart rate cap and a way to track it during exercise — a chest strap or wrist-based monitor both work. If you haven't calculated it yet, do that first: everything in this plan is built around staying at or below that specific number, not a pace or distance target.

You'll also want a baseline MAF test before week 1 — a simple time trial (see below) at your MAF heart rate, on a course you can repeat. Without this starting number, you won't have anything to compare your week 4 and week 8 results against.

Unlike a generic cardio plan, the structure here is built around the testing schedule as much as the training volume — every 2 weeks gives enough time for a genuine adaptation to show up in your numbers, without testing so often that normal day-to-day variation (sleep, stress, weather) muddies the signal.

Weeks 1–2: finding your pace

The goal isn't fitness yet — it's discovering how slow "MAF-slow" actually is for you, and getting comfortable staying there even when it feels too easy or, for a deconditioned aerobic base, surprisingly hard.

Sample week

Monday: 25 min at or below MAF heart rate (walk/run mix if needed)
Wednesday: 30 min, same
Saturday: 35 min, same
Other days: rest or light stretching

Many people are genuinely shocked by how much walking their MAF cap requires in week 1, especially if they've been training primarily by pace or feel rather than heart rate up to this point. This is exactly what an underdeveloped aerobic base looks like — it's the reason the method exists, not a sign it's the wrong approach for you.

Weeks 3–4: extending duration, first real test

Session length increases while frequency holds or ticks up slightly. At the end of week 4, run your second MAF test — the same course, same protocol as your baseline — and compare the time directly.

Sample week

Monday: 35 min
Wednesday: 40 min
Friday: 35 min (add this 4th session if recovery allows)
Saturday: 45 min
Other days: rest

A faster time at the same heart rate here — even a modest one — is the clearest early confirmation that the plan is working. A flat or slightly slower result at week 4 isn't a failure; four weeks is often too early to see the full effect, particularly for anyone starting from a low aerobic base.

Weeks 5–6: consistency, second checkpoint

By this stage the goal shifts from "getting used to the pace" to simply accumulating consistent volume at your MAF cap. Test again at the end of week 6.

Sample week

Monday: 40 min
Tuesday: 40 min
Thursday: 45 min
Saturday: 50 min
Other days: rest or strength training

Weeks 7–8: consolidating the base, final checkpoint

Sessions extend to 45-60 minutes and 4 sessions per week becomes the standard. Run your third MAF test at the end of week 8 and compare it against both your baseline and your week 4 result — a consistent downward trend in time (or upward trend in pace) across all three checkpoints is the strongest evidence the plan is working.

Sample week

Monday: 45 min
Tuesday: 50 min
Thursday: 50 min
Saturday: 60 min (long session)
Other days: rest or strength training

Reading your MAF test results

Three checkpoints (baseline, week 4, week 8) give you a trend line, which matters more than any single number. A single flat or slightly slower test can simply reflect a bad night's sleep, heat, or an off day — it's the direction across multiple tests that tells you whether the aerobic base is actually developing.

  • Consistent improvement each test: the plan is working as intended — continue building volume or extend to a second 8-week block.
  • Improvement at week 4, plateau by week 8: common, and often means it's a reasonable point to hold volume steady for a few more weeks rather than continuing to add duration.
  • No improvement across any test: double-check you're genuinely staying at or below your MAF cap during sessions (a monitor reading a few beats high consistently can hide real progress) and that sessions are actually happening 3-4 times a week, not less.

If you fall behind

Missing a single session isn't a problem — continue the week's schedule as planned. If you miss a full week, pick back up at the same phase rather than repeating it. If you miss 3 or more consecutive weeks, drop back to the start of the previous phase, since aerobic adaptations fade measurably after a month without stimulus.

Common mistakes in a MAF plan

  • Drifting above the heart rate cap to "feel like you're training." This is the single most common way people accidentally turn MAF training into regular easy running, which defeats the purpose.
  • Testing on different courses each time. Makes your data noisy enough that real progress can be invisible — pick one course and stick with it for all three checkpoints.
  • Quitting after a disappointing week 4 test. Four weeks is genuinely too short for some people, especially those starting from a very low aerobic base — the week 8 test is often where the trend becomes clear.
  • Adding speed work too early. Introducing harder sessions before week 8 dilutes the aerobic stimulus the plan is built around and makes your MAF test results harder to interpret.

Frequently asked questions

How many days a week should I do MAF training?
3-4 sessions per week is the typical range for beginners building an aerobic base. Because MAF sessions are capped by heart rate rather than intensity, most people can train more frequently than they could with harder workouts — 5-6 sessions per week is common for experienced MAF athletes, but 3-4 is a realistic and effective starting point.
What is a MAF test and how often should I do one?
A MAF test is a fixed course or distance completed at your MAF heart rate cap, with your time or pace recorded. It's the primary way to track progress in MAF training, since the whole point is to get faster at the same heart rate over time. Testing every 2 weeks on the same course, under similar conditions (weather, time of day, terrain), gives the clearest signal.
My pace is really slow at my MAF heart rate — is that normal?
Yes, and it's one of the most common reasons people quit MAF training too early. If your aerobic base is underdeveloped, your pace at your MAF cap can be surprisingly slow at first — sometimes requiring walk breaks even for regular runners. This is normal and temporary. Resist the urge to run faster than your heart rate allows; the slow pace is what's building the adaptation.
What if I miss a week of the plan?
Continue from where you left off rather than trying to make up missed sessions. If you missed a single week, resume at the same phase. If you missed 3 or more weeks, drop back to the start of the previous phase before continuing, since aerobic adaptations fade without consistent stimulus.
Can I do MAF training for cycling instead of running?
Yes — MAF training works for any steady-state aerobic activity, including cycling, rowing, swimming, and incline walking. The heart rate cap and progression structure stay the same; only the activity and, for the MAF test, the specific course or protocol you use to measure progress changes.
Do I need to test on the same course every time?
Ideally, yes. Comparing a flat, cool-weather test to a hilly, hot-weather test will make your data noisy and can make genuine progress look like a plateau or regression. Pick a course you can return to repeatedly — a track, a flat park loop, or a treadmill at a fixed incline all work well specifically because conditions stay constant.
What happens after week 8?
Most people continue MAF training for 3-4 months total before reintroducing higher-intensity work, since aerobic base development is a slow, cumulative process that doesn't fully plateau in 8 weeks. Week 8 is a reasonable checkpoint to assess your MAF test trend and decide whether to keep building base or start layering in some faster sessions.
Not medical advice. Consult a doctor before starting a new exercise programme, especially if you have cardiovascular concerns, are significantly deconditioned, or have a history of heart disease. Stop exercise and seek medical attention if you experience chest pain, dizziness, or severe shortness of breath.
