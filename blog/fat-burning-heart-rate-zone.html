Fat Burning Heart Rate Zone: What It Is, How to Calculate It, and How to Use It

📅 June 2026 · Updated October 2026 ⏱️ 15 min read 🔥 Fitness

You've seen the "fat burning zone" on a treadmill screen. It's real — but also one of the most misunderstood concepts in fitness. This guide covers what the fat burning heart rate zone actually is, how to calculate yours by age, whether it works for weight loss, and how to train in it effectively.

What is the fat burning heart rate zone?

The fat burning zone is the exercise intensity at which your body uses fat as its primary fuel source. It's generally defined as 60–70% of your maximum heart rate, which corresponds to Zone 2 in a standard 5-zone training model.

At this intensity, oxygen supply keeps up with demand — meaning your body can fully metabolise fat through aerobic pathways. Go harder, and the fuel mix shifts toward carbohydrates, which burn faster but produce more fatigue byproducts (lactate).

This doesn't mean fat burning stops at higher intensities. It shifts. At 60–70% max HR, roughly 60–65% of calories come from fat. At 80–85%, fat contributes around 30–40%. Higher intensity burns more total calories, but proportionally less from fat.

The fat burning zone vs Zone 2 — same thing?

Yes. In a 5-zone model, Zone 2 (60–70% max HR) is identical to what treadmill displays call the "fat burning zone." Zone 2 is the terminology used in endurance sports and sports science. Fat burning zone is the consumer-facing label for the same intensity range.

How to calculate your fat burning heart rate zone

There are two methods: the simple percentage method and the more accurate Karvonen method.

Method 1: Simple percentage (quick estimate)

Step 1: Estimate your max heart rate: 220 − your age

Step 2: Multiply by 0.60 and 0.70 to get your fat burning zone range.

Example for a 45-year-old: Max HR = 175 bpm. Fat burning zone = 105–123 bpm.

  • Tanaka (208 − 0.7 × age) comes from a 2001 meta-analysis of 351 studies plus a laboratory validation. It agrees with 220 − age around 40, reads lower in your 20s, and reads noticeably higher after 60 — which is why older adults often find the standard chart feels too easy.

All three are population averages. Any one person's true max can sit 10–15 bpm away from every formula, so treat the band as a starting point and confirm it with the talk test below.

Method 2: Karvonen method (more accurate)

The Karvonen formula accounts for your resting heart rate, which makes the resulting zones more individual.

Formula: Target HR = Resting HR + (Heart Rate Reserve × intensity %)

Where Heart Rate Reserve (HRR) = Max HR − Resting HR.

Example: Age 45, resting HR 62 bpm. Max HR = 175. HRR = 113.

  • Lower end (60%): 62 + (113 × 0.60) = 62 + 68 = 130 bpm
  • Upper end (70%): 62 + (113 × 0.70) = 62 + 79 = 141 bpm

Notice this is significantly higher than the simple method (105–123 bpm). That's because the Karvonen method accounts for the fact that your heart is already beating at 62 bpm at rest — your working range above rest is what matters, not the raw percentage of max.

The talk test — no heart rate monitor needed

In the fat burning zone, you should be able to hold a full conversation without gasping. Complete sentences, not fragments.

If you can only manage a word or two between breaths, you've gone too hard. If you could comfortably sing, you may be below Zone 2. Full sentences, slightly laboured — that's the target.

Is my heart rate in the fat burning zone? Three real questions

Most people don't want a formula — they want to know whether the number on their watch counts. These three questions come up again and again, and each one shows a different reason the answer isn't as simple as the treadmill chart suggests.

"Is 99 bpm fat burning at 48?"

On the standard chart, a 48-year-old's zone is 103–120 bpm, so 99 bpm is just under it — very light, upper Zone 1. Tanaka gives 105–122 bpm, the same verdict. But for a woman, the Gulati formula puts the zone at 98–115 bpm, and 99 bpm is inside it. When you're within a few beats of the edge, the formula you pick decides the answer. If 99 bpm feels like brisk walking where you can still talk in full sentences, it's doing the job either way.

"Is 130 bpm fat burning for a 55-year-old man?"

By percentage of max, no: the standard zone is 99–116 bpm and Tanaka's is 102–119 bpm, so 130 bpm is roughly 79% of estimated max — moderate, Zone 3. Now add a resting heart rate of 60. The Karvonen zone becomes 123–134 bpm, and 130 bpm sits comfortably inside it. The two methods aren't contradicting each other so much as measuring different things: Karvonen works on the range above your resting rate, so its numbers always run higher. If you use Karvonen, stick with Karvonen — don't mix a Karvonen target with a percentage-of-max chart.

"What's the fat burning zone for a 70-year-old?"

The standard chart says 90–105 bpm, Gulati says 87–101 bpm for women, and Tanaka says 95–111 bpm. That spread of over 20 bpm is the widest of any age on the chart, because 220 − age tends to underestimate max heart rate in older adults. Two practical points matter more than the formula at this age: blood-pressure medications such as beta-blockers blunt heart rate, which makes any age-based target read too high, and the talk test becomes the more reliable guide. If you take heart-rate-lowering medication, ask your doctor what range is appropriate for you.

Does the fat burning zone actually work for weight loss?

This is where the nuance matters — and where the treadmill label is a little misleading.

The fat burning zone burns the highest proportion of calories from fat. But because the total calorie burn per minute is lower than at higher intensities, the absolute amount of fat burned per session isn't necessarily greater.

A useful comparison: a 45-minute moderate-intensity run at 75% max HR will likely burn more total fat than a 45-minute walk at 60% max HR — even though the walk has a higher fat-burning proportion per calorie.

So why does it matter?

Because duration, not intensity, is where the fat burning zone wins. You can sustain Zone 2 for 60, 90, or even 120 minutes. That's not realistic at Zone 3 or 4. More time in the zone = more total fat burned over the session.

There are also longer-term adaptations that make the fat burning zone particularly valuable:

  • Mitochondrial development: Zone 2 training drives the creation of new mitochondria — the structures that burn fat. Over months, your body becomes significantly more efficient at using fat as fuel, even at higher intensities.
  • Fat oxidation capacity: Consistent Zone 2 training increases the rate at which your muscles can oxidise fat, making fat loss easier over time — not just during workouts.
  • Recovery compatibility: Lower-intensity sessions accumulate less fatigue, allowing more frequent training without overtraining. More total volume = more total calories burned.
  • Cardiovascular base: Aerobic base development from Zone 2 makes higher-intensity work more effective when you do it — it's not either/or.

Common mistakes when training in the fat burning zone

Going too hard

This is the most common mistake. Most people, especially those who exercise regularly, find that true Zone 2 pace feels uncomfortably slow. If you're used to running or cycling at a moderate effort, Zone 2 may feel like you're barely moving. This is normal — and correct. If the effort feels genuinely easy, you're probably in the right place.

Not using a heart rate monitor

Perceived effort alone is unreliable for hitting Zone 2. Many people think they're in the fat burning zone when they're actually in Zone 3 — the "moderate" zone that's too hard for the long-duration benefits of Zone 2 but too easy for meaningful high-intensity stimulus. A chest strap or optical heart rate monitor removes the guesswork.

Treating all cardio as Zone 2

Zone 2 isn't the only useful training zone. A balanced approach includes 80% of training volume in Zone 2, with roughly 20% at higher intensities (Zone 4–5). The mistake is spending most time in Zone 3 — which is too hard to be Zone 2 and not hard enough to be Zone 4. It's the least productive zone for most goals.

Expecting rapid results

Mitochondrial adaptations from Zone 2 training take 8–12 weeks to meaningfully develop. The fat burning zone is a long-term investment in your aerobic system — not a quick fix. Most people who stick with consistent Zone 2 training for 3 months notice a significant improvement in endurance and a reduction in effort at any given pace.

How to structure fat burning zone training

For most people, the following framework works well:

  • Frequency: 3–5 sessions per week in the fat burning zone
  • Duration: 45–90 minutes per session (longer is better for fat loss, but start where you are)
  • Modality: Anything that allows sustained, controllable intensity — walking, jogging, cycling, rowing, swimming
  • Intensity check: Use a heart rate monitor; the talk test as a backup
  • High intensity: Add 1–2 Zone 4–5 sessions per week for overall fitness — don't do all Zone 2
  • Resistance training: Pair with 2–3 strength sessions per week to preserve muscle mass during fat loss

What to do if Zone 2 feels too easy on foot

Many runners find that true Zone 2 pace requires slowing to a walk — at least initially. This is fine. Walking briskly at 60–65% max HR is Zone 2. The goal is the heart rate, not the activity. As your aerobic base improves, the same heart rate will correspond to a faster pace.

Fat burning zone training over 40 and 50

The fat burning zone is particularly well-suited to people over 40. High-intensity training carries higher injury risk as recovery capacity declines with age, and the hormonal response to intense exercise changes. Zone 2 provides meaningful cardiovascular and metabolic benefit with minimal injury risk.

For fat loss over 40, research consistently supports the combination of Zone 2 cardio with resistance training. Zone 2 improves fat oxidation and cardiovascular health; resistance training preserves (or builds) muscle mass, which maintains metabolic rate. Together, they're more effective than either alone.

One practical note: max heart rate declines with age (roughly 1 bpm per year after 20), so your fat burning zone range shifts downward. A 55-year-old's fat burning zone is around 99–116 bpm — which may correspond to a moderate walking pace rather than a jog. That's completely fine — the physiological stimulus is the same.

In your 70s and beyond, the standard chart becomes the least reliable of the three formulas: at 75 it gives 87–102 bpm while Tanaka gives 93–109 bpm. If the standard range feels almost too easy to register as exercise, the Tanaka column is likely closer to your real zone — and the talk test is the tiebreaker.

The science behind fat oxidation in Zone 2

Understanding why the fat burning zone works requires a brief look at cellular metabolism. Your muscle cells have two primary ways to produce ATP (the energy currency your body runs on): aerobic metabolism (using oxygen) and anaerobic glycolysis (without oxygen). At low to moderate intensities, aerobic metabolism dominates — and fat is the primary substrate.

The key cellular structures are mitochondria — the organelles responsible for aerobic energy production. Regular moderate-intensity training increases mitochondrial content and fat-oxidation capacity in previously untrained people within weeks. Much of the popular enthusiasm for Zone 2 traces back to work by Dr. Iñigo San Millán, who argues that this intensity best matches lactate clearance and fat use in the muscle.

The evidence doesn't support the stronger claim that Zone 2 is uniquely effective, though. A 2025 narrative review in Sports Medicine (Storoschuk, Gibala and colleagues, "Much Ado About Zone 2") concluded that current evidence doesn't show Zone 2 to be the optimal intensity for mitochondrial or fat-oxidation adaptations, and that harder efforts drive these signals at least as strongly. Zone 2's real advantage is practical: it's easy enough to do for long durations, several times a week, without piling up fatigue — so you can accumulate far more total training at it.

Fat burning zone vs HIIT: which is better for fat loss?

This is probably the most commonly asked question — and the answer is "it depends on what you're optimising for."

The case for the fat burning zone

  • Sustainable for 60–120 minutes, accumulating more total fat burned per session
  • Very low recovery cost — you can train 5–6 days per week
  • Builds the aerobic base that makes all training more effective over time
  • Lower injury risk, suitable for beginners and older adults
  • Drives meaningful mitochondrial adaptation over 8–16 weeks

The case for HIIT

  • Burns more total calories in less time (20–30 minutes vs 60–90 minutes)
  • Creates a larger post-exercise calorie burn (EPOC) lasting up to 24 hours
  • Improves VO2max more rapidly in trained individuals
  • Better for cardiovascular fitness in time-limited scenarios

The optimal approach

Research on elite endurance athletes — who have exceptional fat-burning capacity — consistently shows an 80/20 distribution: 80% of training time in Zone 2, 20% in high-intensity zones. This "polarised" model outperforms spending most time in moderate zones (Zone 3) for both performance and metabolic health.

For most people, this means 3–4 Zone 2 sessions per week plus 1 HIIT session. Avoid the trap of spending all your time in Zone 3 — hard enough to feel like work, but not producing either the mitochondrial adaptations of Zone 2 or the cardiovascular gains of true high intensity.

Fat burning zone training: women-specific considerations

Women's physiology interacts with Zone 2 training in some important ways that are underrepresented in generic fitness advice.

A max heart rate formula built from women

The 220 − age rule was drawn largely from data on men. The Gulati formula (206 − 0.88 × age), developed from 5,437 women, predicts a lower max heart rate for women at every age — so the fat burning band it produces runs about 3–8 bpm below the standard chart. It's shown in its own column in the age chart above, and the calculator at the top of this page uses it when you select Female.

Hormonal cycle and training zones

Heart rate responses vary across the menstrual cycle. In the luteal phase (days 14–28), resting heart rate is typically 2–5 bpm higher and perceived effort at the same intensity is elevated. Women tracking their zones should expect their fat burning zone to feel slightly harder in the second half of the cycle — this is normal and not a sign of declining fitness.

Fat oxidation advantage

Research shows women oxidise a higher proportion of fat at any given exercise intensity compared to men — particularly at moderate intensities. This means Zone 2 training may produce even more pronounced fat-burning adaptations in women, making it a particularly effective strategy for female fat loss goals.

Perimenopause and menopause

Declining oestrogen during perimenopause and menopause shifts fat storage patterns toward visceral (abdominal) fat. Zone 2 training specifically improves visceral fat metabolism and insulin sensitivity — making it arguably more important for women in this life stage than at any other time. Combined with resistance training to preserve muscle mass, consistent Zone 2 work is one of the most evidence-backed interventions for metabolic health after 40.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fat burning heart rate zone by age?
The fat burning zone is 60–70% of estimated max HR (220 minus age). At 30: roughly 114–133 bpm. At 40: 108–126 bpm. At 50: 102–119 bpm. At 60: 96–112 bpm. At 70: 90–105 bpm. Women using the Gulati formula land about 3–8 bpm lower. These are estimates — the Karvonen method with your resting HR gives a more personal range.
Is the fat burning zone the same as Zone 2?
Yes. In a 5-zone training model, Zone 2 (60–70% of max HR) is the same intensity range called the fat burning zone. The terms are interchangeable — Zone 2 is the endurance sports terminology, fat burning zone is the consumer label.
How long should I train in the fat burning zone?
Sessions of 45–90 minutes are typical. Unlike high-intensity training, Zone 2 can be sustained for extended periods. For meaningful aerobic adaptation, aim for at least 45 minutes. Even 30-minute sessions are beneficial — start where you are and build duration gradually.
Does training in the fat burning zone reduce belly fat?
Spot reduction isn't possible — fat is lost from throughout the body in response to a calorie deficit. However, regular Zone 2 training combined with a modest calorie deficit is effective for overall fat loss, including visceral (belly) fat, which responds well to consistent aerobic exercise.
What happens if my heart rate is too high during fat burning zone training?
If your HR exceeds 70% of max, you shift into Zone 3 or higher. The fuel mix shifts toward carbohydrates, and fatigue accumulates faster. You won't get the same mitochondrial and fat oxidation adaptations as true Zone 2. Slow down until you're back in range.
Can I do fat burning zone training every day?
Yes — Zone 2 is low enough in intensity that daily sessions are sustainable for most people. Many endurance athletes train Zone 2 five or six days per week. That said, rest or very easy days are still useful, especially if you're also doing higher-intensity sessions or resistance training.
How do I know if I'm actually in the fat burning zone?
The most reliable method is a heart rate monitor showing 60–70% of your max HR. The talk test is a good secondary check: you should be able to speak in full sentences without gasping. If you're breathing too hard to hold a conversation, you've gone above Zone 2. Perceived exertion should feel "easy" to "moderate" — if it feels like a real effort, slow down.
Is the fat burning zone the same for running, cycling, and walking?
The heart rate target is the same regardless of activity. However, the pace or effort required to hit that HR differs significantly by activity and fitness level. Running typically produces a higher HR at any given effort compared to cycling. Swimming runs 10–15 bpm lower than land activities. The key is always the heart rate number, not the pace or activity type.
What is the difference between the fat burning zone and HIIT?
The fat burning zone (Zone 2, 60–70% max HR) burns a higher proportion of calories from fat and is sustainable for long durations. HIIT burns more total calories per minute and creates a larger post-exercise calorie burn. For fat loss, total calorie deficit determines the outcome — both approaches work. The most effective programmes combine primarily Zone 2 with occasional HIIT sessions.
Does the fat burning zone change for women?
On the standard 220 − age chart the targets are the same, but the Gulati formula — developed from 5,437 women — predicts a lower max heart rate, which puts a woman's fat burning zone about 3–8 bpm lower (for example 102–120 bpm instead of 108–126 bpm at 40). Women also oxidise a higher proportion of fat at moderate intensities, and heart rate runs slightly higher in the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle — normal, and not a sign of reduced fitness.
How long until I see results from fat burning zone training?
Measurable aerobic improvements (improved pace at the same heart rate) typically appear after 6–10 weeks of consistent training. Fat loss results depend on overall calorie balance, not training intensity alone. Most people notice improved energy, reduced resting heart rate, and better recovery within 3–4 weeks of consistent Zone 2 training.
What is the fat burning zone for a 70-year-old?
By the standard 220 − age chart it's 90–105 bpm; for women the Gulati formula gives 87–101 bpm, and the Tanaka formula gives 95–111 bpm. The standard formula tends to underestimate max heart rate in older adults, so Tanaka is often closer. If you take beta-blockers or other heart-rate-lowering medication, age-based numbers don't apply — use the talk test and ask your doctor for a target.
Is 130 bpm in the fat burning zone for a 55-year-old?
Not by percentage of max: a 55-year-old's standard zone is 99–116 bpm, so 130 bpm is about 79% of estimated max — moderate, Zone 3. With the Karvonen method and a resting heart rate of 60, the zone becomes 123–134 bpm and 130 bpm is inside it. Pick one method and use it consistently.
Is 99 bpm a fat burning heart rate?
It depends on age and sex. For a 48-year-old the standard zone is 103–120 bpm, so 99 bpm is just below it (very light). For a 48-year-old woman using the Gulati formula the zone is 98–115 bpm, so 99 bpm counts. On the standard chart, 99 bpm falls inside the zone from about age 55 to 79.
What should I eat before training in the fat burning zone?
For sessions up to 60 minutes, many people train fasted or with a light snack. For longer sessions (75–90 minutes), a small carbohydrate-containing meal 1–2 hours before is helpful. Zone 2 is sustainable enough to run without aggressive fuelling, unlike high-intensity work. Stay hydrated — dehydration raises heart rate and can push you out of Zone 2 without feeling harder.
Not medical advice. Heart rate calculations are estimates. Individual responses vary. If you have cardiovascular concerns, consult a doctor before beginning an exercise programme. Stop immediately if you experience chest pain, dizziness, or difficulty breathing.
