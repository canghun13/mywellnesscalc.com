What is a One Rep Max (1RM)?

Your one rep max is the maximum amount of weight you can lift for a single repetition with good form. It's the standard way to measure absolute strength in resistance training, and it's used to program training loads for strength, hypertrophy, and power.

Testing your true 1RM carries injury risk — especially for beginners. That's why 1RM estimation formulas exist: you lift a submaximal weight for multiple reps, and the formula predicts your maximum. These estimates are accurate to within about 5% for reps under 10.

How to use your 1RM for training

Once you have your estimated 1RM, you can set training loads as a percentage of that number. Different percentages target different training goals:

  • 50–60%: Warm-up, technique work, active recovery
  • 60–70%: Muscular endurance (15+ reps)
  • 70–80%: Hypertrophy / muscle building (8–12 reps)
  • 80–90%: Strength development (3–6 reps)
  • 90–100%: Maximum strength, competition lifts (1–3 reps)

The formulas explained

Epley: weight × (1 + reps / 30) — most widely used, tends to be accurate for moderate rep ranges

Brzycki: weight × 36 / (37 − reps) — accurate for lower rep ranges (1–10)

Lander: (100 × weight) / (101.3 − 2.67123 × reps) — validated in research studies

Lombardi: weight × reps^0.10 — tends to give higher estimates, good for higher rep ranges

Average: Mean of all four formulas — reduces outlier bias

For best accuracy, use a weight you can lift for 3–8 reps with good form. Estimates become less reliable above 10 reps.

Deadlift 1RM is typically the highest of the three main lifts because it uses the most muscle mass and has the shortest range of motion relative to load. Bench press is usually the lowest, since it relies more heavily on smaller upper-body muscle groups. If your ratios differ significantly from these patterns, it often points to a specific strength or mobility limitation worth addressing.

Why your actual lift might not match your calculated 1RM

It's common to test a true 1RM and find it lower — or occasionally higher — than the number this calculator gave you. That gap almost always traces back to one of a few specific causes:

What you noticeLikely causeWhat to do
Missed the lift at the exact predicted weightNeural fatigue from the warm-up sets, or the working set was closer to true failure than it feltRest 3–5 min between heavy warm-up sets; stop 1 rep short of failure on your test set
Actual max is well below the estimateRep set used to estimate was above 10 reps — formula error grows with rep countRe-test using a set of 3–6 reps for a tighter estimate
Actual max is well above the estimateFormula bias (e.g. Brzycki underestimates high-rep sets) or unusually high fast-twitch fiber ratioUse the "Average of all" option, or the Lombardi formula for sets over 8 reps
Estimate feels right on some lifts, off on othersTechnique-limited lifts (e.g. squat depth, bench pause) don't follow the same fatigue curve as simpler liftsTrust the calculator less for technical lifts; weight it more for machine or single-joint movements
Number was accurate last month, feels wrong nowThe estimate is only as fresh as the set it came from — strength shifts with training phase, sleep, and stressRecalculate from a recent set rather than reusing an old number (see FAQ on retesting frequency)

None of this means the calculator is unreliable — formula-based estimates are a starting point for programming, not a substitute for how a specific lift actually feels on a given day. Treat the number as the center of a range, not a fixed target.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is a 1RM calculator?
1RM estimates are generally accurate to within 5–10% when based on a set of 3–8 reps with good form. Accuracy decreases above 10 reps as fatigue and muscle endurance become more significant factors. Use multiple formulas and take the average for a more reliable estimate.
Should I test my actual 1RM?
True 1RM testing carries injury risk, particularly for beginners or with compound lifts. Estimation from a submaximal set is safer and accurate enough for programming purposes. If you do test your 1RM, always use a spotter, warm up thoroughly, and have several months of consistent training behind you.
What percentage of 1RM should I use for hypertrophy?
Research supports a range of 60–85% of 1RM for hypertrophy (muscle building), typically performed for 6–15 reps per set. This covers the full spectrum from moderate loads with higher reps to heavier loads with lower reps — both produce similar muscle growth when taken close to failure.
How often should I test my 1RM?
Strength increases fastest with beginners, so recalculating every 4–6 weeks is reasonable. Intermediate lifters might reassess every 8–12 weeks. A practical approach is to recalculate whenever a set feels significantly easier than expected at a given weight — this often signals a strength improvement.
What's a good 1RM for squat, bench, and deadlift?
It depends entirely on bodyweight, training age, and sex — there's no single "good" number. As a rough guide, an intermediate lifter (1–2 years of consistent training) can typically squat around 1.5× bodyweight, bench 1.0× bodyweight, and deadlift 1.75× bodyweight. Progress relative to your own starting point matters far more than comparing to these general benchmarks.
What's the difference between 1RM and rep max?
1RM (one rep max) specifically means the maximum weight you can lift for exactly one repetition. "Rep max" is the broader term — a 5RM is the maximum weight you can lift for 5 reps, a 10RM for 10 reps, and so on. This calculator estimates your 1RM from any rep max you enter, since testing a true 1RM directly carries more injury risk.
Which 1RM formula is most accurate?
No single formula is universally most accurate — each was validated on different populations and rep ranges. Epley tends to work well across a broad range of reps and is the most commonly used. Brzycki is generally more accurate for lower rep sets (under 10). For the most reliable single number, use the "Average of all" option, which reduces the impact of any one formula's bias.
Can beginners use 1RM percentages for programming?
Yes, but with caution. Beginners' strength improves quickly, so a 1RM estimate can become outdated within just a few weeks, causing percentage-based loads to feel too easy or too hard. Beginners often do better focusing on adding small amounts of weight or reps each session (progressive overload) rather than strictly following percentages calculated from an early 1RM estimate.
Not medical advice. One Rep Max testing and heavy lifting carries injury risk. Always warm up properly, use a spotter for compound lifts, and prioritize form over weight. Consult a fitness professional if you're new to strength training.
