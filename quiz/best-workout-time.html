How much does the time of day actually change?

Less than the confident headlines suggest, and more than zero. When the same people are tested repeatedly across the day, the swing from the morning low to the late-afternoon high is about 7.6% for peak cycling power and 11.3% for average power. That is real, and it is roughly the size of a poor night’s sleep — enough to notice on a test day, rarely enough to decide whether a training block works.

Over months the gap mostly disappears. In a 24-week trial of 42 men training either in the morning or the evening, both groups gained similar amounts of leg strength and muscle size. The morning group also gradually lost its usual morning dip: train at one time of day for long enough and that time starts to become your strong time.

Where timing does seem to make a difference

  • Blood sugar and lipids. In a 2021 randomised trial in overweight and obese men, only the evening training group improved fasting glucose, insulin and blood lipids, although fitness improved equally in both groups.
  • Women and abdominal fat. A 2022 trial in active adults (Frontiers in Physiology) linked morning training to larger reductions in abdominal fat and blood pressure in women, and evening training to better muscular performance in women and lower blood pressure in men.
  • Sleep. Evening training does not harm sleep for most people. The caution applies to hard sessions finishing close to bedtime — in a 2025 study of nearly 15,000 wearable users, sessions ending four or more hours before sleep showed no link to sleep changes at all.

Each of these comes from one or two trials in specific groups, so they work best as tiebreakers. None of them is large enough to outweigh the one factor that reliably decides results: whether you actually train.

Why the quiz weighs your schedule most

The largest differences in outcomes come from training more often, not from training at a better hour. A slot that gets cancelled once a week costs more than any circadian advantage can give back, so the quiz treats the part of your day that is reliably yours as the deciding factor. Your body clock, goal, sleep and training type then shift the recommendation within that frame — and if your days are too irregular to protect any single slot, it recommends anchoring training to a fixed event instead of a clock time.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to work out in the morning or the evening?

Neither is better for most outcomes. Strength and power are measurably higher in the late afternoon — repeated cycle testing puts the daily swing at roughly 8–11% — but trials lasting months find strength and muscle gains end up similar whichever time people train. The time you can repeat week after week matters more than the time of day itself.

Does working out at night ruin your sleep?

Usually not. A 2019 meta-analysis of 23 studies in Sports Medicine found evening exercise did not impair sleep overall, with a possible exception for vigorous exercise ending within about an hour of bedtime. A 2025 study of nearly 15,000 wearable users in Nature Communications found sessions ending at least four hours before sleep showed no link to sleep changes, while a maximal session ending two hours before bed was linked to falling asleep about 36 minutes later than a light one.

What is the best time to exercise for fat loss?

Whichever time keeps you consistent and makes your calorie target easier to hit. Fasted morning sessions burn a higher share of fat during the workout, but 24-hour fat loss is similar when calories are matched. One 2022 trial in active adults linked morning training to larger drops in abdominal fat in women — a single study, so treat it as a tiebreaker rather than a rule.

What is the best time to work out for strength and muscle?

Late afternoon or early evening if you want your best numbers on the day, because body temperature and power output peak then. Over a training block it matters much less: a 24-week trial found similar strength and muscle gains in morning and evening groups. Training at one time also shifts your peak toward it, so if you compete or test at a fixed hour, train at that hour.

Can you change your best time to work out?

Partly. Research on training time shows the body adapts to when you train — people who train in the morning gradually lose most of their usual morning performance dip. Your chronotype (whether you are naturally a morning or evening person) is harder to move, which is why the quiz weighs your schedule and sleep alongside it.
