About this quiz

Almost every guide to coming back from a break answers a single question: how much fitness did I lose? That is the least useful thing to know, because you cannot change it. What you can change is how you return — and the right approach is different for a two-week holiday than for three months on the sidelines after an injury.

This quiz sorts you into one of four comeback approaches based on the four variables that actually alter the answer: how long you were off, what caused the break, what you did during it, and how much training history you had banked beforehand.

Why the cause of the break matters as much as its length

Two people who both missed six weeks are not in the same position. Someone who spent those weeks walking to work and taking the stairs has preserved a surprising share of their aerobic fitness, because research on reduced training consistently shows that maintaining even a fraction of normal activity behaves very differently from complete cessation. Someone who spent the same six weeks recovering from illness has lost more, because the body was spending resources on repair rather than maintenance.

Injury adds a further constraint that no general detraining model captures. The tissue that was injured has its own timeline, and it is usually slower than your cardiovascular recovery. This is why the quiz treats injury breaks separately and defers to medical clearance rather than giving you a number.

The comeback injury window

The single most consistent finding across return-to-training guidance is a mismatch in recovery speeds. Your heart and lungs re-adapt quickly. Your tendons, ligaments and bone do not. That means there is a period — usually the second and third weeks back — where you feel capable of far more than your structure can safely absorb.

Feeling good is therefore not evidence that you are ready to load heavily. It is the expected sensation at exactly the moment the risk is highest. The practical protection is boring but effective: restart at roughly half your previous loads, add volume before intensity, and accept that the first fortnight is about re-establishing the habit rather than proving anything.

What tends to go wrong

  • Testing too early. A time trial or max lift in week one produces a demoralising number and most of the injury risk, while telling you nothing you could not have guessed.
  • Restarting at the old programme. The programme was written for the fitness you had, not the fitness you have. Rescaling is not a setback.
  • Adding intensity before volume. Hard sessions feel like the fastest route back. They are the fastest route to a second layoff.
  • Assuming the loss is permanent. Previously trained muscle rebuilds faster than it originally built, and aerobic losses flatten rather than compounding forever.

Frequently asked questions

How long off training is 'too long'?

There is no cliff edge. Aerobic decline generally becomes measurable somewhere around day 7 to 10, and strength holds for three to four weeks in most people. What changes with length is not whether you can return but how you should return: under two weeks you resume, at four to eight weeks you rebuild aerobic volume first, and beyond eight weeks you treat the comeback as a training block in its own right.

Should I test my fitness on the first session back?

No. A time trial or a max lift in week one tells you almost nothing useful and carries most of the comeback injury risk. Your cardiovascular system recovers faster than your tendons, ligaments and bone, so the session where you feel capable of testing arrives before your connective tissue is ready for it. Wait until you have two to three consistent weeks behind you.

I feel completely fine — do I still need to ease back?

Usually yes, and feeling fine is exactly why. Perceived readiness tracks your heart and lungs, which re-adapt quickly, rather than your tendons and bone, which do not. The mismatch between how good you feel and what your structure can absorb is where most comeback injuries happen, so treat the first one to two weeks as deliberately conservative regardless of how strong you feel.

Does it matter why I took the break?

Considerably. A holiday where you walked daily costs far less than the same number of weeks spent ill in bed, because illness and injury spend recovery resources that would otherwise support adaptation. Breaks caused by injury also carry a specific structural limitation that general detraining guidance does not address, which is why medical clearance should override any quiz or calculator result.

Will I lose all my progress?

Almost certainly not. Detraining losses flatten rather than continuing indefinitely, and previously trained muscle retains additional nuclei that let it rebuild faster than it originally built. Even after long breaks, most people return to their previous level in substantially less time than it took to reach it the first time.
